# Right to education

*EncyKlopedia — private English research draft, linked to UNESCO concept10.*  
**Status:** Research candidate, not reviewed, not published. No canonical KA assertions are created by this text.

## Definition and policy scope

The **right to education** is the human-rights principle that people should have access to education under conditions shaped by public obligations, equality and the aims of education. It is more than an entitlement to a school place: its realization involves educational opportunities, accessibility, appropriate learning conditions and the ability to pursue education without discrimination. This article addresses the policy and institutional dimension of the right, rather than individual teaching methods. [UN—UDHR, art. 26](https://www.un.org/about-us/universal-declaration-of-human-rights); [UNESCO—state obligations](https://www.unesco.org/en/right-education/state-obligations-responsibilities?hub=66813).

The [UNESCO Thesaurus entry for *Right to education*](http://vocabularies.unesco.org/thesaurus/concept10) is the vocabulary anchor for this draft. Its classification and relationships are **not** themselves evidence that the legal, historical or empirical claims below are true.

## International normative foundations

The [Universal Declaration of Human Rights](https://www.un.org/about-us/universal-declaration-of-human-rights) (1948), Article 26, recognizes the right to education. It distinguishes elementary education, which it calls compulsory, from higher education, for which access is framed in terms of merit. It also addresses educational aims and the choice of education by parents. The Declaration is a foundational international declaration; it should **not** be described as a treaty binding every country by itself.

The [International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights](https://2covenants.ohchr.org/About-ICESCR.html), Article 13, articulates obligations of its States parties. It specifies compulsory, free primary education; steps to make secondary education broadly accessible; measures to make higher education accessible on the basis of capacity; and respect for educational choice within minimum standards. These clauses differ by level and by the nature of the required measure. A country-specific statement requires checking the State's treaty status, reservations, laws and implementation at a particular date.

The [Convention on the Rights of the Child](https://www.unicef.org/child-rights-convention/convention-text), Article 28, addresses education as a right of children, calls on States parties to make primary education compulsory and free, and identifies measures on secondary access, attendance and school dropout. Its rights-holder and treaty-party context must not be conflated with that of all adults or with the UDHR.

UNESCO's [Convention against Discrimination in Education](https://www.unesco.org/en/legal-affairs/convention-against-discrimination-education) was adopted in 1960 and entered into force in 1962. It supplies another relevant treaty framework for studying exclusion and equal treatment in education. Its application to a particular country requires separate legal verification.

## What governments are responsible for

UNESCO organizes State obligations in three complementary categories: **respect**, **protect** and **fulfil**. Respect concerns avoiding State actions that obstruct the right; protect concerns preventing interference by third parties; and fulfil concerns measures that enable people to enjoy it. For implementation analysis, UNESCO also describes four interrelated dimensions: **availability**, **accessibility**, **acceptability** and **adaptability**. This framework helps distinguish, for example, having schools available from whether the schools are actually reachable, equitable and suitable for learners. [UNESCO—state obligations](https://www.unesco.org/en/right-education/state-obligations-responsibilities?hub=66813).

In this encyclopedia branch, those dimensions lead to specific questions about who has legal responsibility, how education is funded, how providers are regulated, whether discrimination is prevented, how barriers are addressed and how public authorities respond to inadequate provision. No single governance arrangement should be treated as universal without evidence.

## How progress can be evaluated

The right itself is a normative commitment; an indicator is a measurement of one part of its realization. [UNESCO Institute for Statistics, SDG 4 indicators](https://www.uis.unesco.org/en/methods-and-tools/sdg4-indicators) provides distinct measures including completion rates, out-of-school rates, learning proficiency, parity indices, education financing and years of free or compulsory education guaranteed in law. Each measures a different phenomenon. A high enrolment rate, for example, would not alone establish equitable learning outcomes or non-discrimination; this is an analytical caution, not a finding about a specific country.

Comparative claims must specify country, period, level of education, population, indicator definition and original source. Figures, causal explanations and rankings have **not** been imported into this draft.

## Implementation tensions and limits

Education-policy decisions may involve tensions among equal access, resource allocation, provider choice, inclusion, educational quality and administrative capacity. These are legitimate research questions, but an encyclopedia article should describe evidenced debates rather than presenting a single policy preference as a demonstrated conclusion. A later version should compare country cases, review evidence about effective measures and separate legal guarantees from experienced outcomes.

This draft does **not** yet provide a jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction legal comparison, historical chronology, quantified outcomes, or reviewed counterarguments. It should not be used as a released article until these lacunae and all source references have been examined.
