# Right to education

*English-first encyclopedic research draft · UNESCO `concept10` · provisional `KQ3` · **NOT CANON / NOT FOR PUBLICATION***

## Scope and definition

The **right to education** is a human-rights entitlement recognized in international instruments and expressed through different commitments concerning access, equality, educational aims and public responsibilities. The right should not be reduced to school enrolment: its normative treatment also addresses the conditions of education and the responsibilities of institutions. This article examines the **education-policy dimension**, not a universal legal determination for every country. [RTE-KA-PROP-001, 005, 015–016]

The vocabulary anchor is the [UNESCO Thesaurus concept10](http://vocabularies.unesco.org/thesaurus/concept10), which should be distinguished from sources establishing legal claims or empirical outcomes. Wikidata [Q69370](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Q69370) remains a proposed identity crosswalk, not an approved equivalence.

## International normative foundations

### Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948)

Article 26(1) of the [Universal Declaration of Human Rights](https://www.un.org/en/about-us/universal-declaration-of-human-rights/) states the right and distinguishes free elementary/fundamental education from the obligation of elementary education. Article 26(2) identifies human development and human rights among educational aims, and Article 26(3) protects parental choice. The UDHR is a **declaration**, not a treaty whose ratification could be used to establish a State’s separate treaty obligations. [RTE-KA-PROP-001–004]

### International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (1966)

Article 13 of the [ICESCR](https://2covenants.ohchr.org/About-ICESCR.html) recognizes education as a right and distinguishes primary, secondary and higher education. The text provides for free compulsory primary education while formulating other education-level commitments through access and progressive introduction of free education. Article 2 also addresses progressive realization, resources and non-discrimination. These are **treaty provisions for States parties**; claims about any particular State require its treaty status, applicable reservations, domestic instruments and the relevant date. [RTE-KA-PROP-005–008]

### Convention on the Rights of the Child (1989)

Under Article 28 of the [Convention on the Rights of the Child](https://www.unicef.org/child-rights-convention/convention-text), States parties recognize **children’s** right to education. Primary education is to be compulsory and free; other measures address secondary access, attendance and reduced school dropout. This child-specific treaty should not be conflated with a claim that every educational level is free in every country. [RTE-KA-PROP-009–011]

## Discrimination and equality of opportunity

UNESCO’s [Convention against Discrimination in Education](https://www.unesco.org/en/legal-affairs/convention-against-discrimination-education) was adopted in Paris on 14 December 1960 and entered into force on 22 May 1962. Article 1 describes discriminatory distinctions or restrictions affecting equal educational treatment. Treaty participation and national legal effects require separate examination; neither a historical adoption date nor a treaty provision proves implementation by all States. [RTE-KA-PROP-012–014]

## Translating norms into policy

UNESCO distinguishes duties to **respect**, **protect** and **fulfil** the right, and analyses four essential features: **availability**, **accessibility**, **acceptability** and **adaptability**. These distinctions make it possible to ask different policy questions: do facilities and programmes exist; can people reach them without discrimination; do educational conditions meet acceptable standards; and are systems capable of responding to learners’ circumstances? [RTE-KA-PROP-015–016] See [UNESCO’s account of State obligations](https://www.unesco.org/en/right-education/state-obligations-responsibilities?hub=66813).

An encyclopedic treatment of implementation should therefore connect laws and administrative responsibilities with financing, provision, quality, and remedies. It must not presume that any specific design of public/private provision is the only lawful model without jurisdiction-specific sources. This is an **analytical agenda**, not a conclusion about an individual education system.

## Monitoring and evidence

The [UNESCO Institute for Statistics SDG 4 framework](https://www.uis.unesco.org/en/methods-and-tools/sdg4-indicators) differentiates measures of learning proficiency (4.1.1), completion (4.1.2), out-of-school rates (4.1.4), legally guaranteed years of free/compulsory schooling (4.1.7), parity (4.5.1), and spending per learner (4.5.4). These are **indicator definitions**, not imported country statistics. [RTE-KA-PROP-017–019]

To make country comparisons, a future article must attach population, level of education, geographic coverage, observation year, data vintage and methodology to each empirical assertion. Enrollment cannot by itself establish learning quality, nondiscrimination or compliance with an individual treaty obligation.

## Open research directions

A richer article will eventually investigate changes in national constitutional guarantees, laws and judicial decisions; access barriers affecting specific groups; financing and fee policy; conflict and displacement; implementation mechanisms; and research evaluating programmes. These topics remain **questions to document**, not automatically accepted encyclopedic facts. National examples, causal claims and quantitative comparisons must await primary sources and independent review.
